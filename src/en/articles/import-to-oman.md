## Who this guide is for

This is for the owner of a small company importing for the first time, for the procurement officer who was handed the import file overnight, and for anyone who wants to understand what their customs broker is actually doing on their behalf. It is written from inside the process — by someone who files declarations on Bayan and tracks containers at Sohar — not from reading the regulations alone.

You will come away with three things: a clear sequence from contract to the container leaving the port, a way to calculate what you will really pay to customs, and the list of errors that hold shipments more than any others, with how to avoid each.

## Before you ship: are you actually eligible to import?

The most expensive mistake is a container arriving before your legal eligibility is complete — storage charges start accruing while you are still running between offices. Before shipping, confirm:

1. **A commercial registration that includes an import activity** for your type of goods, issued by the Ministry of Commerce, Industry and Investment Promotion. Goods outside the activities on your registration mean trouble at clearance.
2. **A valid membership of the Oman Chamber of Commerce and Industry.**
3. **Registration on the Bayan system** to obtain an importer code, with either an activated user of your own or an authorised licensed customs broker. The registered importer is legally answerable for the declaration, even when a broker files it.
4. **VAT registration** if your supplies exceed the mandatory threshold. A registered importer recovers import VAT through its return; an unregistered one pays it as a final cost — a 5% difference on the value of every shipment.
5. **Prior permits** if your goods are restricted (see the HS code section). Some permits must be obtained before the declaration is filed, not after.

## The core documents — and what has to match between them

Customs does not read each document on its own; it reads them together and looks for conflicts. The documents you need on almost every sea shipment:

| Document | Issued by | What to check |
|---|---|---|
| Commercial invoice | Exporter | Importer name matches the commercial registration; value, currency and delivery term (Incoterm) are explicit; goods description is specific, not generic |
| Packing list | Exporter | Package count, net and gross weights, dimensions — line by line against the invoice |
| Bill of lading (B/L) or air waybill (AWB) | Shipping line or carrier | Consignee, container number, weight, package count — against the packing list |
| Certificate of origin | Chamber of commerce in the exporting country | Attested, and country of origin matches the invoice |
| Delivery order | Shipping line's agent in Oman | Issued after line charges are paid; without it the container does not leave even after customs release |

> One number that differs between two documents is enough to hold a declaration. 95 packages on the packing list and 100 on the invoice is not a "small difference" to the examiner.

## The 12-digit HS code and prior permits

Oman applies the GCC Common Customs Tariff, and Bayan requires the Harmonised System (HS) code **in its 12-digit form**. A code in the old 8-digit format is rejected automatically — one of the most common rejections among new importers who copy the code from the exporter's invoice as-is.

The code settles three things at once: the duty rate, whether the goods attract excise tax, and whether a regulator's permit is required. The main regulators:

- **Telecommunications Regulatory Authority** for telecom and wireless electronics: type approval first, then a customs release permit for each shipment.
- **Ministry of Health** for medicines and medical supplies.
- **Food Safety and Quality Centre** and the **Ministry of Agriculture, Fisheries and Water Resources** for food, animal and plant products.
- **Conformity requirements** (through the Ministry of Commerce) for certain goods such as electrical appliances, building materials and toys.

Ask the exporter for the code, then verify it yourself in Bayan's import-requirements lookup **before you sign the contract** — not after the container arrives.

## What you will actually pay: duty and VAT

The general rule for non-exempt goods:

- **Customs duty:** 5% of the CIF value (goods + freight + insurance to the port of arrival).
- **VAT:** 5% of (CIF + customs duty).

Example: a shipment with a CIF value of OMR 10,000:

| Item | Calculation | Amount |
|---|---|---|
| Customs duty | 10,000 × 5% | OMR 500 |
| Taxable base | 10,000 + 500 | OMR 10,500 |
| VAT | 10,500 × 5% | OMR 525 |
| **Total paid to customs** | | **OMR 1,025 (≈ 10.25% of CIF)** |

VAT is collected together with duty through Bayan itself. A VAT-registered importer recovers the OMR 525 through its return (or defers it to the return where eligible), so its real cost is OMR 500; an unregistered importer bears the full OMR 1,025.

Cases that change the calculation:

- **Goods of GCC origin**, or goods on which duty was paid at the first point of entry into the GCC: exempt from duty against the statistical declaration (makasa) from that country.
- **Exempt goods** by decree (staple foods, certain raw materials and production inputs): zero duty; VAT follows the classification of the item.
- **Excise goods** (tobacco, energy drinks, carbonated and sweetened drinks): an additional excise tax calculated before VAT.
- **Free zones:** no duty and no VAT while the goods remain inside; moving them into the local market is treated as a full import at their value at the time of transfer.

## Clearance on Bayan: from arrival notice to release

1. **Before arrival:** the shipping line's agent sends the arrival notice. Have your documents attested and the HS code verified. Start the declaration **before** the vessel berths if you can — every day beyond the free period is charged as storage.
2. **Preparing the declaration:** your broker or authorised user creates the customs declaration: importer details, shipment details, line items with codes and values, and the attached documents.
3. **Review and risk routing:** the system reviews the declaration and routes it: direct release, documentary check, or physical inspection of the container. The importer's history, the type of goods and the accuracy of the documents all affect the route.
4. **Payment:** duty and VAT are paid electronically through the system.
5. **Customs release:** release is issued in Bayan. This does not mean the container has left.
6. **Delivery order and gate-out:** you pay the shipping line and port charges, receive the delivery order, and the haulier takes the container out. The empty container goes back to the line within the agreed period or detention charges begin.

Normal timing for a shipment with clean documents: one to three working days from berthing. Anything longer is usually a missing document or a permit that was not requested in time.

## The errors that hold declarations more than any others

From day-to-day practice, these are the recurring causes — all of them preventable before filing:

1. **Conflicts between documents:** quantity, weight or description differing between invoice, packing list and bill of lading.
2. **Importer name that does not match the commercial registration** exactly — an abbreviation or trade name instead of the legal name.
3. **An 8-digit HS code**, or a code that does not fit the goods description.
4. **An unattested certificate of origin:** the declaration may be accepted against a cash deposit refundable when the original is produced within a set period — but you pay up front and chase the refund.
5. **A regulator's permit not obtained** before filing (the Telecommunications Regulatory Authority release permit is the classic case).
6. **Goods outside the activities on your commercial registration.**
7. **An unjustifiably low value** compared with the market — triggering a revaluation and delay.
8. **Invoice currency or delivery term missing**, so the CIF value is estimated, usually not in your favour.

## Checklist before you file

- Commercial registration covers the goods, chamber membership is valid
- Importer name on invoice and bill of lading matches the registration exactly
- Quantity, weight and description agree across invoice, packing list and bill of lading
- Incoterm and currency are stated on the invoice
- HS code is 12 digits and verified by you, not only by the exporter
- Certificate of origin attested by the chamber in the exporting country
- Regulator permits obtained (where required)
- Shipping line charges paid and delivery order confirmed
- Port free-time expiry date known and written down
- Duty and VAT calculated in advance, with funds ready for electronic payment

## A last word

Importing into Oman is not complicated — but it does not forgive carelessness with details. Most delays happen before the container arrives, in a document nobody reviewed or a permit nobody requested. Review, then file.
